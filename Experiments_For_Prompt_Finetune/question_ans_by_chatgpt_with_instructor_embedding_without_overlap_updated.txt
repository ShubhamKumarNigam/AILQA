(1) What is the maximum period that a juvenile can be sentenced for a crime? Are there are any exceptions to this?
Ans:In India, the maximum detention that can be imposed on a juvenile is for 3 years, irrespective of the gravity of the offence committed. However, the Juvenile Justice Act, 2000 immunes children who are less than 18 years of age at the time of the commission of the alleged offence from trial through a criminal court or any punishment under criminal law.

(2) Can a child in India be charged as an adult? How is it different from the law in the United states of America?
Ans:In India, a child under the age of 18 is immune from trial through a criminal court or any punishment under criminal law in view of Section 17 of the Juvenile Act. In the United States, the age to determine juvenility varies from state to state, with most states setting the age at 18, but some states setting it at 17 or 16. Many states permit execution of juveniles of 16 years for murder as an adult and could be tried by criminal courts for prosecution and punishment as adults as per the gravity of the alleged offence.

(3) What other applicable sections of the Indian Penal Code are added in charges pertaining to acts that break the rules enacted in the prevention of money laundering act in India?
Ans:Section 211, 212, 213, and 214 of the Indian Penal Code are applicable sections that are added in charges pertaining to acts that break the rules enacted in the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act in India.

(4) On whom is the burden of proof to prove that his assets were obtained legitimately with respect to the prevention of money laundering act of India?
Ans:The burden of proof to prove that his assets were obtained legitimately with respect to the Prevention of Money Laundering Act of India lies on the person accused of an offence under Section 3 of PMLA, whose property is attached and proceeded against for Confiscation. The person must discharge the onus of proof (Section 24) vested in him by disclosing the sources of his Income, Earnings or Assets, out of which or means by which he has acquired the property attached, to discharge the burden that the property does not constitute proceeds of crime. Where a transaction of acquisition of property is part of inter-connected transactions, the onus of establishing that the property acquired is not connected to the activity of Money-Laundering, is on the person in ownership, control or possession of the property, though not accused of a Section 3 offence under PMLA, provided one or more of the interconnected transactions is or are proved to be involved in Money-Laundering (Section 23).

(5) Give me a summary of the nirbhaya case and the ramifications of it on the Indian legal field.
Ans:The Nirbhaya case was a 2012 gang rape and murder of a 23-year-old woman in Delhi, India. The case sparked widespread protests and led to the passing of the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, which amended the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, and the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. The Act also introduced new laws to protect women from sexual harassment and acid attacks. The Act also introduced the death penalty for rape of a minor under 12 years of age. The case also led to the introduction of fast-track courts to expedite the trial of rape cases.

(6) What is the procedure that the police must follow when they have been informed of a cognizable offence? Please provide relevant sections to bolster the answer.
Ans:The police must register an FIR (First Information Report) immediately if the information given to them discloses a cognizable offence. This is mandated by Section 154 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC). If the information does not disclose a cognizable offence, the police may conduct a preliminary inquiry to ascertain whether a cognizable offence has been committed. This inquiry should not take more than one week. Action must be taken against police officers who do not register an FIR if the information given to them discloses a cognizable offence. Relevant sections include Section 154, 156, and 157 of the CrPC.

(7) Under what circumstances can the court join the charges of an offender pertaining to Indian law?
Ans:According to Section 218(1) of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) of India, for every distinct offence there shall be a separate charge and for every such charge there shall be a separate trial. However, Sections 219, 220, 221, and 223 of the CrPC provide exceptions to this rule, allowing the court to join charges of an offender in certain circumstances.

(8) What happens when there is a mistake during the framing of charges of a criminal defendant in Indian law? Please provide relevant sections of law.
Ans:According to Section 215 & 464 of the Criminal Procedure Code (CrPC) of India, if there is a technical breach of rules in the framing of charges that does not go to the root of the case, the court may reverse the conviction if the accused was informed of the details of the offences committed and thus no prejudice was caused to him. The court may also alter or add to any charge at any time before judgment is pronounced (Section 216 (1) of CrPC). Additionally, the court cannot drop a charge if a person has been charged, and the accused must either be convicted or acquitted (Section 217 of CrPC).

(9) Is DNA evidence admissible in Indian courts?
Ans:Yes, DNA evidence is admissible in Indian courts. In 2005, the Criminal Code of Procedure, 1973 was amended to allow for medical practitioners, after authorization from a police officer who is not below the rank of sub-inspector, to examine a person arrested on the charge of committing an offence and with reasonable grounds that an examination of the individual will bring to light evidence regarding the offence. Section 53 as amended includes the examination of blood, blood stains, semen, swabs, in case of sexual offences, sputum and sweat, hair samples, and finger nail clippings, by the use of modern and scientific techniques including DNA profiling and such other tests which the registered medical practitioner thinks necessary in a particular case. DNA analysis is also of utmost importance in determining the paternity of a child in the cases of civil disputes. Need of this evidence is most significant in the criminal cases, civil cases, and in the maintenance proceeding in the criminal courts under section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. The Hon'ble Supreme Court's acceptance of Delhi High court judgment of ordering Veteran Congress Leader N.D. Tiwari to undergo the DNA test is also an example of the admissibility of DNA evidence in Indian courts.

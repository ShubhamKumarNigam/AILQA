The Joint-Stock Companies Act, 1857, is a law that governs the incorporation and regulation of joint-stock companies and other associations in India, with or without limited liability for their members. The Act begins by stating that it is intended to amend the law relating to the incorporation and regulation of joint-stock companies and to enable members to limit their liability for the debts and engagements of the company.

The Act consists of four parts. Part I deals with the constitution and incorporation of companies and associations. It states that seven or more persons associated for any lawful purpose may form themselves into an incorporated company by subscribing their names to a Memorandum of Association and complying with the requirements of the Act in respect of registration. However, no joint-stock company or association can be formed for the purpose of banking or insurance with limited liability. The Memorandum of Association must contain certain matters, including the name of the proposed company, the part of the territories in which the registered office of the company is to be established, the objects for which the proposed company is to be established, the liability of the shareholders, whether it is to be limited or unlimited, the amount of the nominal capital of the proposed company, the number of shares into which such capital is to be divided, and the amount of each share.

Part II of the Act deals with the management and administration of companies. It states that the company shall have a registered office to which all communications and notices may be addressed. The registered office and the name of the company must be displayed in a conspicuous position in the company's place of business. The company must keep a register of shareholders, which must contain the names, addresses, and occupations of the shareholders, the shares held by each of them, the amount paid on the shares, the date at which the name of any person was entered in the register as a shareholder, and the date at which any person ceased to be a shareholder.

Part III of the Act deals with the winding-up of companies. It states that a company may be wound-up by the court under certain circumstances, such as when the company is unable to pay its debts or when three-fourths of the capital of the company has been lost or become unavailable. The court may appoint a liquidator to collect the assets of the company and distribute them among the creditors and shareholders.

Part IV of the Act deals with the registration of companies. It states that the local government may appoint such registrars, assistant registrars, clerks, and servants as it may think necessary for the registration of companies under this Act. Every person may inspect the documents kept by the registrar of joint-stock companies, and there shall be paid for such inspection a fee of one rupee for each inspection. All costs, charges, and expenses properly incurred in the voluntary winding-up of a company, including the remuneration of the liquidators, shall be payable out of the assets of the company in priority to all other claims.

The Act also contains provisions for the repeal of the earlier Joint-Stock Companies Act, 1850, and for the registration of existing companies under the new Act. The registration of any existing company under this Act shall not prejudice any right which previously to such registration has accrued to any creditor or other person against the company in its corporate capacity in respect of any act done or liability incurred previously to such registration, or against any person then being or having been a member of such company.
